### Section 3: Values & Motivation

#### Q1 — Why do you care about energy?

**Edited answer:**
> Energy is the foundation of any civilization — not as a career argument,
> but as a physical reality. From an early age I understood the universe
> in terms of energy exchange. At university, entropy was my first
> encounter with the connection between philosophy and engineering systems.
> The First Law of Thermodynamics is the one I return to most: energy
> cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed. If civilization wants
> to lift people out of poverty — covering food, housing, healthcare,
> education, security — it needs a reliable, clean energy source. If that
> source is not clean, the consequences reach far beyond any single
> civilization. The planet itself is the carrier for all of us.

**Raw voice material:**
- Early realization: the universe, in all forms, is about energy exchange and transformation.
- Entrance point: entropy at university — connection between philosophy and physical engineering systems.
- Conviction: Energy is foundational to civilization and human dignity.
- Grounding: First Law of Thermodynamics — energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed.
- Consequence: If we want civilization to provide for all people (food, housing, healthcare,
  education, security, mobility), we must source energy cleanly — or we damage the planet
  that carries all life.

**Key quote:**

#### Q3 — Why nuclear and clean gas? What do people get wrong?

**Edited answer:**
> Every energy source has real trade-offs and externalities. The problem
> is that ideology prevents us from seeing them clearly. Nuclear has strong
> safety data — Chernobyl and Fukushima created political damage, but they
> also produced evidence we can learn from. Wind turbines kill birds in
> natural habitats — that's a real environmental cost, not a talking point.
> Solar has manufacturing impacts. Coal pollutes at every stage.
> The engineering question is never "which source do I believe in?"
> It's "what does the actual evidence say, and what are the real costs
> at every stage of the system?" That applies equally to gas extraction —
> if Mexico needs to reduce its dependence on Texas imports, it must do so
> with clean fracking fluids, water recycling, and damage mitigation,
> not with the same practices that caused the problems in the first place.

**Raw voice material:**
- Every energy source has trade-offs and externalities. Ideology blinds us to evidence.
- Nuclear: strong safety data, but Chernobyl and Fukushima created political damage.
- Wind: kills birds in natural habitats — real environmental cost.
- Solar: manufacturing impact. Coal: obvious pollution.
- Engineering question: "What does the evidence say? What are the real costs?"
- Applies to gas extraction too — clean fracking fluids, water recycling, better site selection.

**Key quote:**
> "There are people who strongly defend what they believe, and once that belief is broken,
> they keep defending it anyway. But there is evidence that nuclear is secure. There is
> evidence that climate change is happening. There is evidence of new technologies for
> cleaner energy. Every type of energy has been politically damaged somehow — Chernobyl,
> Fukushima, bird deaths from wind. We have to be very careful with all the externalities.
> I don't pick ideology. I pick evidence."

#### Q4 — What worries you? What do you want to build?

**Edited answer:**

**What worries you:**
- Mexico imports ~75% of its natural gas from Texas/California.
  One geopolitical disruption = energy crisis. Unacceptable for a sovereign nation.
- Decarbonization lag: Mexico currently at ~33% clean energy.
  Target should be 66%+ by 2040–2050.
- Infrastructure fragmentation: dams exist but are inefficient,
  divide communities, not fully leveraged.
- AI, healthcare, electric transport, Industry 4.0 — all demand
  more grid capacity, but the grid is not ready for those fluctuations.

**What you want to build:**

1. **Distributed nuclear baseload** — Four plants (north, south, east, west Mexico).
   Decarbonized, reliable. Fuel via long-term contracts (Australia, France).
   Serves major urban clusters (Guadalajara, León, frontera, Veracruz).

2. **Agrovoltaics** — Solar panels creating shade → small agricultural
   ecosystems (livestock, crops, berries). Dual land use: energy + food, same footprint.

3. **Gravity storage on existing dams** — Use solar during the day to pump water up.
   Release at night through turbines. Solves storage problem.
   Upgrades existing infrastructure without building from scratch.

4. **Clean domestic gas extraction** — Organic fracking fluids (non-toxic),
   water recycling, better site selection.
   Reduce political vulnerability to Texas imports within 10 years.

5. **Grid modernization** — Distributed, multi-directional energy vectors.
   Regional resilience, microgrids.
   Grid strong enough to absorb AI-driven demand growth, EV adoption,
   and political disruptions simultaneously.

**Key quote:**
> "What I really care about is that Mexico imports three-quarters of its natural gas —
> almost all from Texas. That's a political vulnerability we cannot accept. We need to
> extract clean natural gas inside our borders. We also need four nuclear plants distributed
> across the country — north, south, east, west — to provide baseload decarbonized power.
> We need agrovoltaics where solar panels create shade for agriculture. We need to upgrade
> our dams with gravity storage — pump water up during the day with solar, release it at
> night for energy. We have so many projects to do. But we have to do them clean, cheap,
> sustainable, and with the people in mind — not just the grid."
